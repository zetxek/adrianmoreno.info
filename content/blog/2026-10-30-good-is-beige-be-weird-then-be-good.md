The fastest way to become invisible right now is to be _competent_.

Talk is cheap, and I've known that for years 😅. Anyone can fill a meeting, restate the problem in more words, and sound busy while saying nothing. What changed is that being accurate is cheap too. A model will produce eight hundred plausible, correctly punctuated words in about ten seconds, name a framework, hand you three tidy bullets, and land a conclusion. It'll be right.

Which is exactly the problem. "Right" is free now, and free things stop being signals.

---

## Good is the average of everyone else's best practice

When you optimise for "good", you optimise toward the mean. Good is what's left after you sand off everything someone might disagree with. Follow enough best practice and you converge on the same voice as every other person following the same best practice.

You've read that voice. It restates the question, adds a paragraph of context nobody requested, weighs three balanced considerations, and concludes that the answer depends. Flawless, and completely beige.

Now put a model behind it. The model has read all of it, every beige article and every breathless LinkedIn "win", and when you ask it to improve your draft it will move you confidently toward the middle, because the middle is what "good" looks like on average.

This is why I've stopped asking models whether my writing is any good. They agree with everything. **"You're absolutely right" is the most expensive sentence in modern work**, and it bills you in taste, one polite confirmation at a time.

---

## Weird is unique. Weird on its own is noise.

You can't just decide to be weird, though. Weird without good is another flavour of slop, and we already have an infinite supply of that.

You need both at once, weird and good, and that's much harder, because the two instincts pull in opposite directions. Good wants the safest phrasing. Weird wants the true one.

Imagine two posts about the same idea. The first is technically perfect and could have been written by anyone with a style guide. The second starts with a sentence you'd only say out loud to a colleague you trust, has one paragraph that goes further than it strictly needs to, and ends somewhere slightly uncomfortable. The second one is the only one anybody remembers. It's also the one that could be wrong.

And that risk is what makes it worth reading.

---

## Personality attracts. It also repels. Good.

This goes well beyond writing. Who hires you, who buys from you, who wants to work next to you: all of them are choosing between options that look roughly the same on paper. Same skills on the CV, same features on the pricing page, same "passionate about quality" on the About page. The thing that tips the decision is whether they remember you, and nobody remembers the option that was fine for everybody.

That's what positioning is. You choose who you're for, and in doing so you choose who you're not for. Optimise for the average customer, the average hiring manager, the average colleague, and you're optimising for someone who doesn't exist. The average is a statistical artefact, like averaging a Copenhagen January and a Spanish August and getting a pleasant spring day that never happens.

Personality does both jobs at once. The same trait that makes the right client think "finally, someone who gets it" makes the wrong one quietly close the tab. That rejection is the differentiation working. A company that wants a safe pair of hands for a safe project should hire someone else, and you both win when they figure that out from your first conversation instead of your third month.

A model can't make this choice for you. It aims at the middle by design, and the middle is exactly the space where everyone is interchangeable. Deciding who you're for is a decision, and decisions are the part you can't delegate.

So be the obvious yes for the few, and an easy no for the rest. The rest were going to forget you anyway.

---

## What authenticity actually costs

Authenticity gets treated as a personality trait, something you either have or you don't. To me it feels more like a bill that keeps arriving. Mine usually has four lines:

- **An opinion you'd defend in a room.** A position with your name on it, one somebody could push back on. If nobody could disagree with what you wrote, you didn't say anything.
- **A specific that cost you something.** A real number, a real constraint, the thing that went badly. Models can write "significant improvement" for free. Only you can write what actually happened.
- **Willingness to be mildly disliked.** A percentage of readers will always want the beige version. That's the tax, and it's cheaper than being forgettable.
- **Consistency about what you keep saying.** A voice is a set of arguments you keep coming back to. Start every piece from scratch and what you end up with is a content calendar.

*(The giveaway in most AI-assisted writing is that nothing in it cost the author anything. The vocabulary is the least of it.)*

---

## How I use the tools without sounding like one

1. **First draft stays mine.** Bullet points, fragments, half sentences, wrong order. The mess is where the point of view lives, and it's the part I refuse to outsource.
2. **Give the model my position, not a topic.** "Sharpen this argument" produces something. "Write about authenticity" produces beige.
3. **Ask it to argue against me.** Agreement is worthless. I want the strongest counter-case, so the version I publish has already survived contact.
4. **Read it aloud.** Anything I wouldn't say to a person gets cut. This catches more slop than any prompt.
5. **Keep one unfixable thing per piece.** A phrase, a reference, a joke that only lands if you know me. The Spanish sayings I translate literally, even though they make no sense in English. That's the fingerprint, and a model will happily sand it off.

---

To be clear, I like that the baseline went up. If you used to produce incoherent text and now produce mild, average paragraphs, that's a real improvement. Sloppy was never the goal.

But when everyone can be accurate, accuracy stops being the differentiator, and **personality** becomes the only thing you can't generate on demand. You are the part of the work that a prompt can't reach.

So be good. Genuinely, properly good. Obsessively good in whatever matters to you. Then, be specific enough that only you could have written it, and specific enough that it could fail. Being right is now table stakes. Being someone in particular, for someone in particular, is the whole game.
